\begin{abstract}
We implement K-means (Lloyd's algorithm) from scratch and apply it to the
UNSW-NB15 network-intrusion dataset, comparing a Euclidean objective against a
damped-diagonal Mahalanobis objective. On a class-balanced, z-standardized
subsample we select $k=10$ by elbow and silhouette, evaluate cluster--class
agreement in-sample via majority-vote accuracy, precision, recall and F1 plus
internal silhouette/V-measure, and compare both runs against a scikit-learn
reference. The two geometries produce near-identical partitions; per-feature
damping removes heavy-tailed influence without changing the label-based outcome,
and we argue that any within-geometry silhouette gain is tautological.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem \& Dataset}
UNSW-NB15 (Moustafa \& Slay, 2015) is a dataset for classification of network
intrusions. It contains a benign class and nine attack classes. The dataset has
175{,}341 rows and 47 columns; after dropping non-feature and string columns we
are left with an augmented feature matrix of 39 numeric features.

This codebase stratifies the data to produce a more workable dataset and
standardizes the features. This becomes relevant in later discussions of the
simplified Mahalanobis distance, and means our evaluations must be read in the
context of the sample size and the data-processing choices. In addition to the
base project, we apply damping during the simplified Mahalanobis distance
calculation, discussed below. Results can be reproduced with the
\texttt{make reproduce} command.

\section{Implementation}
\texttt{KMeansScratch} implements Lloyd's algorithm with no library K-means call
(the \texttt{no\_library\_kmeans\_in\_scratch} check is clean):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Initialization} (\texttt{\_init\_centroids}): \texttt{random}
    draws $k$ distinct rows; \texttt{kmeans++} seeds the first centroid
    uniformly, then each next centroid with probability proportional to its
    squared distance to the \emph{nearest} chosen centroid, with a uniform
    fallback if every point coincides with a chosen centroid (zero total
    $D^2$). Both use the project distance so the seeding matches the objective.
  \item \textbf{Assignment} (\texttt{\_assign}): each point to its nearest
    centroid (argmin of the $(n,k)$ squared-distance matrix).
  \item \textbf{Update} (\texttt{\_update}): each centroid moves to the mean of
    its members. Empty clusters are kept in place (their old centroid is
    retained) rather than re-seeded: this avoids consuming the RNG stream
    mid-loop (which would break reproducibility of the restart budget) and did
    not occur in our converged runs.
  \item \textbf{Convergence} (\texttt{fit}): the loop stops when the Frobenius
    (L2) norm of the centroid shift is $< \texttt{tol}$ ($10^{-4}$) or at
    \texttt{max\_iter} (300). scikit-learn's \texttt{KMeans} also uses an
    \emph{absolute} centroid-shift test, but it aggregates the shift as the sum
    of squared per-centroid changes and compares that sum to \texttt{tol},
    whereas we use the L2 norm of the full shift matrix. The two are closely
    related but not identical, so the implementations can settle at marginally
    different local optima (a small, explainable source of the reference
    residual in Section~\ref{sec:eucl-mahal}). An absolute shift test is well
    scaled here because the z-standardized features are unit-variance, so
    centroid magnitudes are $O(1)$.
  \item \textbf{Restarts}: $n_{\text{init}} = 50$ restarts; the restart with the
    lowest inertia is kept (raised from 10 to 50 to improve reference agreement
    on this high-dimensional, multi-modal dataset; see
    Section~\ref{sec:eucl-mahal}).
\end{itemize}
The scikit-learn \texttt{KMeansReference} is used only as an oracle
(\texttt{compare\_to\_reference: true}); the submission runs on
\texttt{scratch}.

\section{Choosing $k$}
\subsection{Euclidean}
Over $2 \le k \le 15$, $k=10$ was selected as optimal. The elbow test shows
inertia following a relatively smooth curve with an inflection around
$8 \le k \le 11$; the silhouette score shows a clear peak at $k=10$. Ten is a
reasonable point under both tests, and it matches the ten true classes.

\section{Evaluation}
To go from clusters to classifications we use majority vote: every point in a
cluster is assigned the majority true class within that cluster.

Evaluations were performed only on the data used to fit the model, which limits
what we can say about generalization. We nonetheless work with what we have, and
additionally inspect principal-component projections to see how tightly the
clusters appear. These visual checks should be taken with a grain of salt, but
they support the quantitative results.

\subsection{Similarity between metrics}
In general the Euclidean and Mahalanobis distances behaved very similarly. As
examples, both had accuracy exactly $0.3271$, and the silhouette scores (not
adjusted for the distance metric used) were $0.4577$ for Euclidean and $0.4573$
for Mahalanobis. When scored in each run's own (adjusted) geometry, the
Mahalanobis silhouette rises to $0.4832$, consistent with the feature scaling
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:eucl-mahal}.

As a side note, the only notable difference between the two metrics is inertia,
which should not be compared across distance metrics.

The small difference between the two metrics can mean a couple of things:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The data is largely normalized, so metrics that account for the high
    variance of certain dimensions have a small effect.
  \item The data is noisy and the clusters happen to sit in shapes that do not
    affect the partition. For example, a high-variance, unnormalized dimension
    far from the other clusters would still cluster fine even if it carried a
    large share of the inertia.
\end{itemize}
We find that most features lie in relatively similar ranges, but a few are much
larger; this likely drives the higher within-geometry silhouette under
Mahalanobis. Euclidean distance is the primary criterion for the evaluation
sections that follow.

\subsection{Agreement with sklearn}
Compared against the scikit-learn clusters, our Euclidean ARI is $0.90$ and our
damped-Mahalanobis ARI is $1.0$. Together these indicate a well-aligned
reference for the Euclidean run and a clean implementation check for the
Mahalanobis run. The ARI of $1.0$ for the Mahalanobis run is explained by the
reference being fed the same geometry: scikit-learn's \texttt{KMeans} is
Euclidean-only, so we pre-scale each feature by $\sqrt{c_f}$, which makes its
Euclidean objective identical to our damped-diagonal-Mahalanobis objective; both
then minimize the same function and land in the same optimum. The ARI of $0.90$
for the Euclidean run, where the reference is a genuinely independent
implementation on the same data, shows high agreement, with the small residual
attributable to the different centroid-shift convergence test. This is a good
indicator of the in-sample correctness of the implementation.

\subsection{Metrics}
The model's accuracy is far above random, which is good. When choosing between
macro and micro precision/recall we consider our stratification: most classes
are well balanced, with a few rare attack classes, so we report macro figures to
weight the rare classes more heavily.

Macro precision and recall are very similar across the two metrics; for
Euclidean they are $0.3560$ and $0.3107$ respectively. Recall is the more
important of the two here, because it indicates the clustering is
under-sensitive. The associated macro F1 is $0.2522$, which is also quite low.
The principal-component view below shows how heavily the feature groups overlap,
which would sensibly hurt performance.

\section{Euclidean vs.\ Mahalanobis}\label{sec:eucl-mahal}
As discussed, there are very few differences in evaluation between the
Euclidean and Mahalanobis metrics, possibly because most features are similar in
scale to one another. The discussion below holds the sample (3{,}730 points),
$k$ ($=10$), the random seed, and the restart budget ($n_{\text{init}}=50$)
fixed; only the distance changes. Features are used in their fixed CSV column
order (the 39 numeric columns), and the diagonal weights are applied positionally
to that order.

Most features were already similar in scale and variance, but a few had large
skew and variance, which is a good reason to use a Mahalanobis metric. Applying
the Mahalanobis metric effectively reduces the feature variance, lowering the
chance that highly variable features dominate the clustering. Our simplified
(diagonal) Mahalanobis metric does not, however, account greatly for skew, which
motivates the additional per-feature scaling below.

We introduce an additional level of processing by damping six outlier-heavy
features. The table below describes their distributions: we see some very high
maximum $|z|$ values and heavily skewed data. $c_f$ is the damping factor
applied to each feature.
\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{Damping factors $c_f$ and distribution statistics of the six damped features.}
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    Feature & $c_f$ & $z$-skew & max $|z|$ \\
    \midrule
    stcpb, dtcpb & 0.05 & 1.2 / 1.2 & 2.5 / 2.6 \\
    sbytes & 0.30 & 27.9 & 34.0 \\
    rate & 0.10 & 3.2 & 4.9 \\
    sload & 0.10 & 5.4 & 14.0 \\
    dload & 0.20 & 8.6 & 12.4 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

An important consideration is that, because we scale these features down, we
obtain inherently better within-geometry silhouette scores, and some other
evaluations will look better. This is not evidence of a better clustering: a
K-means partition is compact in its own metric by construction, so a
within-geometry silhouette gain is tautological and is not counted as an
improvement. We have no held-out data to measure generalization, but the damping
is a reasonable way to reduce outlier-driven distortion.

\section{Conclusion}
It is important to remember that no non-training data was used for evaluation and
only a fraction of the original dataset was used. While the principal-component
visualization suggests clear shapes for some clusters, there could be
differences unaccounted for that would appear if the rest of the dataset had been
included in evaluation or training.
